\subsection{Deriving likelihood}

We assume we have a dataset of $N$ unrelated individuals.

Start by denoting $\boldsymbol{Y}$ as a vector of length $N$ standardised phenotypes. We define $\boldsymbol{X}$ as a matrix where $x_{i_k}$ as the number of singletons that individual $i$ carries in bin $k$. The singleton counts are observed directly. Bin $k$ contains singletons of age $t_k$ generations old. Note that this makes the simplifying assumption that all singletons within a bin have the same age, which is the midpoint of the bin. If we use a large number of small bins, this should be a reasonable simplifying assumption.

An individuals phenotype can be given as:
\begin{equation}
    y_{i} = \mu + \sum_{k} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_{ik}} \beta_{kj} + e_i
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the population mean phenotypic value, $\mathcal{S}_{ik}$ is the set of singletons that individual $i$ carriers in bin $k$. $\beta_{kj}$ is the effect size for singleton $j$ in bin $k$ and $\beta_{kj} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_k^2)$.

All effects and residuals are be mutually independent. Since the singleton effects have mean of zero, and the variances of independent terms are additive, we can sum the variance terms across bins:

\begin{equation}
    y_{i} | x_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}( \mu, \sum_{k} x_{i_k} \sigma_{k}^{2} + \sigma_{e}^{2}).
    \label{eq:conditional}
\end{equation}

We aim to estimate the parameter $s$ and $\sigma_{m}^2$. $\sigma_{m}^2$ is the variance contributed by a single mutation before selection has had a chance to act upon it. $s$ is the rate at which $\sigma_{m}^2$ variance decays over time. Specifically, the variance contributed for mutations of age $t$ can be given as:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{k}^2 = \sigma_{m}^{2} e^{-\lambda{t_{k}}}
    \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}

    y_{i} | x_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}( \mu, \sum_{k} x_{i_k} \sigma_{m}^{2} e^{-\lambda{t_{k}}} + \sigma_{e}^{2})
\end{equation}

Because each singleton is carried by exactly one individual, no effects are shared between individuals, and the residuals of unrelated individuals are assumed to be independent. The phenotypes are therefore independent conditional on $\mathbf{X}$, and the likelihood is:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{N}( \mu, \sum_{k} x_{i_k} \sigma_{m}^{2} e^{-sT_{k}} + \sigma_{e}^{2})
\end{equation}

\subsection{Priors}

We place the prior for $\sigma_m^2$ on the mutational heritability,
$h_m^2 = V_M / V_P$, where $V_M = U \sigma_m^2$ is the mutational variance, $U$
is the number of new mutations per individual per generation, and $V_P$ is the
phenotypic variance ($V_P = 1$ for a standardised phenotype). THerefore, the priors for each parameter is:
\begin{align}
    \mu &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 0.1^2) \\
    \sigma_e^2 &\sim \mathcal{N}^{+}(0,\, 1) \\
    \log_{10}(h_m^2) &\sim \mathcal{N}(-3,\, 1),
        \qquad \sigma_m^2 = \frac{h_m^2\, V_P}{U} \\
    s &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 0.03^2)
\end{align}
where the second argument of each normal distribution is a variance and
$\mathcal{N}^{+}$ denotes a normal distribution truncated to positive values.
The prior on $s$ is centred on zero and allows negative values, so that a posterior concentrated above zero gives evidence of selection.

\section{Version without bins}

If each singleton is instead assigned its own age $t_{ij}$, the variance becomes
\begin{equation}
    v_i = \sigma_m^2 \sum_{j=1}^{x_i} e^{-s t_{ij}} + \sigma_e^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ is the total number of singletons carried by individual $i$. The binned model is the approximation in which $t_{ij}$ is replaced by $t_k$ for every singleton in bin $k$. The sum depends on $s$, so it must be calculated over every singleton in the sample at each evaluation of the likelihood. In practice we therefore use the binned model with many narrow bins. Because the number of parameters does not depend on $K$, increasing $K$ carries no statistical cost.

\section{Power to detect $V_M$ and $\lambda$}

We would like to know, for a given $\lambda > 0$ and $\alpha$ significance level, what is the chance our model returns a significant result. Power = $\Phi\frac{(\lambda_{true}}{SE} - z_{a}$. Therefore, we need to estimate SE($\hat{\lambda}$).

\subsection{Step 1}

As before, we denote a vector of standardised phenotypes as $y_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, v_{i})$, where $v_{i} = \sigma^2_m \sum_{k} C_{ik} f(\lambda, t_{k}) + \sigma^2_e$. When we write it this way, $\lambda$ appears inside a sum with one term per age bin, which makes it challenging to see how the precision of $\hat{\lambda}$ depends on the design. Therefore, we would like to linearise the equation. So, for the purposes of estimating power, we will use two approximations to derive an approximation for the $SE(\lambda)$

First, we replace each participants distribution of singleton ages by a single summary statistic - their mean singleton age: $$\bar t_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_k C_{ik} t_k, \qquad T_i = \sum_k C_{ik}$$, so that $\sum_k C_{ik} f(\lambda, t_k) \approx T_i\, f(\lambda, \bar t_i)$.

Second, we approximate $1/(1+\lambda t) \approx 1-\lambda t$

Both approximations are given by the produce of $\lambda \bar t$, which is 0.017 at the plausible value $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ and the observed mean singleton age of 17.4 generations.

If we substitute our approximations, the variance for individual $i$ becomes:

\begin{equation}
    v_{i} \approx \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_m^2 T_{i} - \lambda \sigma_m^2 (T_{i} \bar{t_i})
\end{equation}

Therefore, the variance is now a simple weighted sum of two quantites with known measurements for each participant: how many singletons they carry $T_i$ and that count multiplied by their mean singleton age $T_{i} \bar(t_i)$.

\subsection{Step 2}

The previous step showed that estimating our model is approximately equivalent to regression $y^2_i$ on two variables measured for each participant. To know the precision of that estimate, we need the variance in its outcome $y^2_i$. Since $y_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, v_{i})$, writing $z_i = \frac{y_i}{\sqrt{v_i}}$ gives a standard normal variable and rearranging gives $y_i^2 = v_i z_i^2$. The square of a standard normal has variance 2, so:

$$\mathrm{Var}(y_i^2) = 2 v_i^2$$

It is worth noting that the standard deviation of $y_{i}^2$ is $\sqrt{2}$ times it's own mean - so a single participants squared trait value is am extremely imprecise estimate of their trait variance under this model. The uncertainty is larger than the quantity being estimated.

\subsection{Step 3}

In a multiple regression setting, the precision of a coefficient relies on the variation that predictor explains after account for all other variables in the model. Our two variables are singleton count $T_{i}$ and the product of $T_{i} \hat{t_i}$. These are strongly correlated.

We can write each persons singleton age as a cohort-wide average plus an individual deviation from that average, $\bar t_i = t_0 + \delta_i$, where $t_0$ is the average across participants and $\delta_i$ has mean zero. We can multiply through by $T_{i}$ to obtain $$T_i \bar t_i \;=\; \underbrace{t_0 T_i}_{\text{a multiple of } T_i} \;+\; T_i \delta_i$$.

The first term is just $T_{i}$ multiplied by a constant and so doesn't contribute to $\lambda$. Only the second term $T_{i} \delta_i$ is informative. This has a straightforward interpretation. If every participant’s singletons were on average the same age, then selection would have reduced every participant’s singleton contribution by the same proportion. That is indistinguishable from mutations simply having smaller effects when they arise — a smaller $\sigma^2_m$. No amount of data could separate the two explanations. The strength of selection is identified only by participants differing from one another in the typical age of the singletons they carry.

Treating $T_i$ and $\delta_i$ as independent, the informative variation in the second variable is

$$\mathrm{sd}(T_i \bar t_i \mid T_i) \;\approx\; \bar T \times \mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)$$

where $\bar T$ is the average singleton count and $\mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)$ is the standard deviation across participants of their mean singleton age, in generations.

\subsection{Step 4}

Using the formula for the standard erroer of an ordinary regression coefficient, we can susbitute in the residual variance from Step 2 and the informative variation from Step 3:

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2v^2}{N \,\bar T^2\, \mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)^2}} \;=\; \frac{v\sqrt{2/N}}{\bar T \cdot \mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)}$$

Since $\beta = -\lambda\sigma^2_m$, $$\mathrm{SE}(\hat\lambda) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2}{N}} \cdot \frac{v}{\sigma^2_m \bar T} \cdot \frac{1}{\mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)}$$

The middle factor has a direct interpretation. The proportion of trait variance attributable to singletons is

$$h^2_{\text{sing}} = \frac{\sigma^2_m \bar T \bar f}{v} \;\approx\; \frac{\sigma^2_m \bar T}{v}$$

where $\bar f$ is the average value of $f(\lambda, t)$ across the observed ages, close to 1 when singletons are young. The middle factor is therefore $1/h^2_{\text{sing}}$, giving

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{SE}(\hat\lambda) \;\approx\; \frac{\sqrt{2/N}}{h^2_{\text{sing}} \cdot \mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i)}\;}$$

Expressing the spread in mean singleton age relative to its own average, $\mathrm{sd}(\bar t_i) = t_0 \times \mathrm{CV_{age}}$, recovers the form used in the main text. We note that these two factors are not independent quantities: their product is simply the absolute spread in mean singleton age across participants, in generations, and only that product has meaning.

The expression identifies three ways to improve precision: increase the cohort size $N$, increase the proportion of trait variance carried by singletons, or increase the spread across participants in the age of the singletons they carry. Only $N$ enters as a square root; the other two enter directly, so a twofold gain in either is worth a fourfold increase in sample size.


\subsection{Batching}

Now we have the formula for the precision of $\lambda$, we now consider a model where, to improve power, we split individuals into $B$ equally sized batches. Singletons would be ascertained within batches, but their dates inferred from the entire cohort. I propose this will improve power for several reasons, listed below.

\subsubsection{Increasing $h^2_{sing}$}

Under the neutral coalescent model, the expected site frequency spectrum is $\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}] = \frac{\theta}{i}$, where $\zeta_{i}$ is the number of sites with a derived allele count of $i$ and $\theta = 4N_{e} \mu L$. So $\mathbb{E}[\zeta_1] = \frac{\theta}{1} = \theta $. Therefore, the number of segregating singletons is a function of $N_{e}$, the effect population size of the total population, not $N$, the number of indviduals you have sampled.

A $MAC = 1$ variant is carried by exactly one person. If we have $\theta$ singleton sites and $n/2$ people to share them, then each person has on average $\frac{\theta}{n/2} = \frac{2\theta}{n}$ singletons per person. Therefore, the number of singletons an individual carried doubles if the sample fraction is halved.
